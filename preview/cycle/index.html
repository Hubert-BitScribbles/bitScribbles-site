    Where it started

    With a simple request: something easier than the popular period apps, which can feel like a research project. When we asked around, we heard the same thing again and again. Period apps aren't good, so most people use a paper calendar. And almost everyone still wanted a tool.

    Research says much the same. The popular apps are built around pregnancy and treat perimenopause as an exception (Massey University, 2025). They make a wrong guess hard to correct (University of Washington, 2017). Their predictions seem to assume a textbook 28-day cycle, and drift by up to 8 days for anyone less regular (Women's Health, 2021). And in 2025 a US jury found that Meta had illegally collected data from users of Flo, one of the most popular period apps (The Register).

    What paper does well

    A paper calendar never guesses wrong, never judges a long cycle, keeps everything private, and shows the whole year at a glance. The app keeps those qualities and adds what paper can't: the pattern over months and years, a fair forecast that says how sure it is, and a heads-up.

    So the main screen does one job. It shows where you are and offers one button, "My period started today", with "an earlier day" one tap away. Every date can be changed later.

    Forecasts that say how sure they are

    Every forecast comes with a word: Fairly sure, Likely, Rough guess or Can't say, and a range that widens to match. It depends on how many recent periods are logged, how much they vary, any unanswered gaps, and how far ahead the date is.

    Forecasts only need the day a period starts, so logging the end is optional, and the "Still going?" reminder can be switched off in Settings. Try switching "Predictions use" to "What I entered" in Settings, the gear on Today.

    When cycles change

    Cycles change at both ends of the years with periods, and at times in between. The app isn't for trying to conceive, but it should feel right through all of these:

    • First periods. Cycles are often irregular for the first few years. About 90% fall between 21 and 45 days, and by the third year most are 21 to 34 days (ACOG, 2015).
    • After a pregnancy. Without breastfeeding, periods usually return six to eight weeks after birth. With it, most within six months, some up to a year (1177, Sweden's health service).
    • Coming off hormonal contraception. A first normal period usually comes about a month after the withdrawal bleed, and it can take longer after the injection (Tommy's).
    • Perimenopause. Cycles start to vary by 7 days or more, then gaps grow past 60 days (STRAW+10 staging). Menopause is usually counted from 12 months without a period.

    People also link changes to illness, stress or travel, which tags can record. A chosen pause, such as pregnancy or hormonal contraception, is set aside rather than counted as a gap.

    One setting, Changing cycles, covers all of these: less forecasting, more record. Today shows the days since your last period and your longest recent gap instead of a countdown. History shows each cycle against your old usual. If you like, you can say why things are changing; it only changes the wording and the help you see, and nothing leaves the phone. Around perimenopause, a period after 12 months without one comes with a plain note to have it checked, as doctors advise. Try Changing cycles at the top of Today, then Settings, the gear on Today.

    Research worth following

    • Massey University's study of tracking apps across three life stages: teens starting their periods, people after birth, and perimenopause. A three-year project, reported in August 2025.
    • University of Washington, 2017: what users found missing in period apps.
    • Women's Health, 2021: how far app predictions drift from real cycles.
    • JMIR, 2026: menopause apps, their promise and their gaps.

    Details, only if you want them

    Flow and discomfort are for a single day; skip any day and nothing is lost. Tags and a note are for the whole cycle, such as travel or a new job. There are a few starter tags, and you add your own. History can search them, or show only the cycles with one tag, so you can spot your own patterns.

    Some people wonder whether the moon or living with others affects their cycle. The evidence for syncing is weak, and for the moon it's unsettled, so the app makes no claims either way.

    Years of records, gaps included

    A missing record assumes nothing. After a much longer gap than usual, the app asks once whether a period went unlogged. "I didn't have one" counts as a long cycle; "I didn't log it" or "Not sure" leaves a marked gap that's shown but left out of the numbers. History shows recent cycles, a row per year and a timeline of every period.

    Help where you live

    The Support tab lists health lines, sexual health services, youth services and information for each stage, for your own province or country, each checked against the service's own site and dated.

    Finding free period products in the moment is a different job, for a different moment, so it belongs to its own tool, run by the community groups who know their area. Support links to one where it exists, and nothing from your record goes with you.

    Make it yours

    Accurate matters, but so does wanting to open it. Settings offers a few looks, and each one has some fun with the same record:

    • Ritual: candlelight and ceremony. Candles light one by one through the cycle, and a period is "the rite".
    • Garden: leaves grow through the cycle, and period days bloom.
    • Pixel: an old game screen, where each day is a block and a period is a quest.
    • Book: ruled paper and red ink, under the name Chapters. Every cycle a page, every stage a chapter, and a period is "turn the page".
    • Discreet: looks like an ordinary calendar app, for anyone whose phone gets seen by others.

    Plain stays the default. A look never changes the record, the safety notes, Support or the doctor's summary. Looks could one day be shared as simple files of colours, pictures and words, never code, so a shared look can't touch anyone's data. Try them from the gear on Today.

    Your record is yours

    Settings keeps everything about the record in one place: an encrypted backup and restore, export in an open format to move to another private app, import from Clue, Flo or another app (read on the phone, with a preview of what comes across and what's left behind), and delete everything.

    The summary for a doctor is one page: typical cycle, range, period length, a chart of cycle lengths over time and a table of recent cycles, with flow and discomfort, and tags and notes only if you tick them. A name line is left blank to fill in by hand, because the app never stores your name. It's the same whatever look you use, and it's saved or printed by you, never sent.

    Private by design

    No accounts, no ads, no analytics or trackers. Your record stays on your phone, backups are encrypted with a passphrase only you know, and it works offline. It's not a medical app and it never marks "safe days". Nobody else sees your record: not partners, not us.

    The name

    The working name is Tender. It means sore, and it means gentle and kind: the body, and going easy on yourself, in one plain word.

    About 450 periods in a lifetime. Go easy on yourself.
    And reach out when you need to: a friend, someone you trust, or the help lines in , there any time.

    Other names we tried, and why we set them aside. These come from quick searches in October 2026, not a trademark search.

    • Circa, Latin for "around", as in "expected around 15 Nov". Already a registered US trademark for an anxiety app, and used by other health and sleep apps.
    • Ebb, as in ebb and flow. Already the name of a privacy-first period tracker.
    • Ritual, for something repeated and cared for across a life. Already a women's vitamin brand, a Canadian food-ordering app and a cosmetics brand.
    • Ritu, Sanskrit for season and the right time, and an old word for a period. Already an offline period app in Nepal, among several similar-sounding apps.
    • Rtu, the same word without its vowel. It reads as an acronym, and in buildings it means a rooftop unit.
    • Moon names and Phases, for the lunar month and life's phases. The most crowded names among period apps, and the app makes no claims about the moon.
    • Red Letter and other red names, for the days you mark on a calendar. Red names are already common among period apps: RedCalendar, RedBird, RedMode and Ruby.
    • Flow, for a period and for a calm, absorbed state. Too close to Flo, the biggest app in the category.
