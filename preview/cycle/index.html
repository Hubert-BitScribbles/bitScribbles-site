    How "near me" stays private

    1. Everyone gets the same list. The app carries Project AIM's list of boxes, which is public and identical for every phone. When AIM updates it, phones download the new copy. Downloading it says nothing about who you are or where you are.

    2. The phone does the sums. With "Use my location", the phone compares its own position with the list and sorts the boxes. The location is never sent anywhere and isn't saved.

    3. Or just pick a town. Anyone who'd rather not share location with the app at all chooses a town instead, as in the mockup.

    4. Directions only when asked. Tapping Directions opens the person's own maps app. Nothing else on the page loads a map from another company.

    5. Hosts choose how precise. A box at a library can show its address. A box at someone's home can show the street or block, so no one's house becomes a pin on a map.

    Details, only if you want them

    Each day: flow and discomfort change from day to day, so during a period Today offers them for that day only. Skip any day and nothing is lost. History sums up each period ("heavy on day 2, then light"), which is the record someone might take to their own doctor.

    Each cycle: tags and a note describe the whole stretch, such as travel or a new job. There are a few starter tags, and people add their own in their own words. History can search tags and notes, or show only cycles with one tag, with their typical length beside the overall one. That's how a person spots their own patterns.

    What's usual, what's changing, what's ahead

    My usual (in History) lets anyone who knows their cycle enter it by hand. It's used until three cycles are logged, and they can choose to keep predicting from it. History shows the typical length (the middle value, so one odd cycle doesn't pull it), the range and any cycle well off it, without judging it.

    Planning ahead (on Today) takes a date, such as a trip or a race, and says whether a period is likely then. Ranges widen the further ahead they go.

    Some people wonder whether the moon or living with others affects their cycle. The evidence for syncing is weak, and for the moon it's unsettled, so the app makes no claims either way.

    Years of records, gaps included

    A missing record assumes nothing. When a period is logged after a much longer gap than usual, the app asks once whether one went unlogged. "I didn't have one" counts as a long cycle, which matters most around perimenopause. "I didn't log it" or "Not sure" leaves a marked gap that's shown but left out of the numbers. A chosen pause, such as pregnancy or hormonal contraception, is set aside.

    Trends say how complete they are, and predictions use recent cycles, since cycles change with age. History shows recent cycles in detail, a row per year, and one timeline of every period across the years. Try the gap question in History.

    Forgetting to tap, and fixing dates

    If a period runs past the person's usual length without its end being logged, Today asks "Still going?" and offers the likely end days, so one tap puts it right. "Change dates" moves the start or end of the latest period a day at a time, and older ones are changed from History. In the mockup, start a period, then use "skip ahead to day 7" to see it.

    Support where you live

    The Support tab lists health lines, sexual health services, youth services and information for each stage, for the person's own province or country. Every number and link is checked against the service's own site, dated, and rechecked before each release, as in Clear Tracker. They're links and phone numbers only, so nothing is shared by opening them. Project AIM and joni could suggest what belongs there.

    The tracker and the boxes, kept apart

    Finding a box doesn't need the tracker. Someone can open the app just for Free products without logging anything. Nothing about a person's cycle is ever linked to the boxes they look at, and none of it leaves the phone.

    "Box empty?" lets someone tell Project AIM a box needs restocking. In the app it would open a ready-made message in their own messages or email, with the box's name and nothing else. They choose whether to send it.

    The same promises as the rest of the app

    No accounts, no ads, no analytics or trackers. Records stay on the phone, backups are encrypted by the person who makes them, and the app works offline once installed. Project AIM, joni and bitScribbles never see anyone's record.
